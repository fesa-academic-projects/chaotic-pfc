\begin{abstract}
The insertion of finite impulse response (FIR) filters in the feedback
loop of chaotic generators is a known strategy for bandwidth limitation in
chaos-based communication systems (CBCS). Although the filter does not
prevent master-slave synchronization, its interaction with the nonlinearity
of the generating map may destroy the chaotic behavior, so the presence of
chaos must be verified for each design. This work investigates, by means of
numerical simulations, the conditions under which chaos is preserved in the
two-dimensional H\'enon map with internal FIR filters, considering four
filter types (lowpass, highpass, bandpass, and bandstop) and seven design
windows, including a sweep of the Kaiser shape parameter. Points of the
order-cutoff plane whose orbits become unbounded are classified as
divergent. The remaining points are classified as chaotic when the largest
Lyapunov exponent, which measures the average exponential rate at which
nearby orbits separate, is positive, and as periodic otherwise.
The results show that filtering does not eliminate the chaotic regime but
reorganizes it in the parameter space in a way that depends on both the
filter type and the design window. Therefore, the spectral specification of
the filter alone does not ensure a chaotic generator, and the dynamics must
be verified for each design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================
% [Items 6, 7, 8, 9] Paragraph 1: chaotic signals and their use as carriers
\IEEEPARstart{D}{eterministic} discrete-time chaotic signals are bounded in
amplitude, aperiodic, and exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions
(SDIC), so that orbits starting arbitrarily close separate, on average
exponentially fast~\cite{Alligood1996}. They also have a broadband spectrum
and a noise-like appearance, which motivates their use as carriers in
communication systems~\cite{Baptista2021}. Among the advantages usually
attributed to such carriers are the difficulty of interception by
unauthorized receivers, which places them among the physical-layer security
approaches~\cite{Mukherjee2014}, the spreading of the transmitted spectrum,
and, for some modulation schemes such as differential chaos shift keying
(DCSK), robustness to multipath fading~\cite{Kaddoum2016}. This
interest grew after Pecora and Carroll~\cite{Pecora1990} showed that two
identical chaotic systems can be synchronized despite SDIC, when the
receiver is driven by state variables of the transmitter.

% [Items 10, 11, 12] Paragraph 2: how a CBCS transmits information
In chaos-based communication systems (CBCS), the message is embedded in a
chaotic carrier at the transmitter and recovered at the receiver. In
coherent CBCS, the receiver synchronizes a replica of the transmitter
generator with the received signal and uses it to extract the message.
Non-coherent architectures, in contrast, recover the information from
statistical or structural properties of the received signal without a
synchronized replica~\cite{Kaddoum2016}. Examples of CBCS include chaotic
modulation schemes~\cite{Baptista2021}, image coding and
encryption~\cite{Sabatini2020}, systems based on three-dimensional
attractors~\cite{Souza2019}, and phase-locked loops for systems based on
chaotic attractors~\cite{Silva2025sbrt}.

% [Items 13--18] Paragraph 3: band limitation, filter in the loop, four types
The broadband spectrum of chaotic signals, however, conflicts with the fact
that practical channels are band-limited. If the spectrum of the transmitted
signal is not confined to the channel passband, the distortion introduced by
the channel prevents the receiver from synchronizing, and the message is
lost~\cite{Eisencraft2011}. To overcome this limitation, Eisencraft
\textit{et al.}~\cite{Eisencraft2009} proposed inserting a discrete-time
linear filter in the feedback loop of the chaotic generator, and Fontes and
Eisencraft~\cite{Fontes2016} showed that the filter does not prevent
synchronization, a result already indicated in~\cite{Eisencraft2009}.
Further practical limitations, such as finite-precision digital
implementation, were investigated by Borges and
Eisencraft~\cite{Borges2021sbrt}. The lowpass case corresponds to a baseband
channel, but other spectral masks arise in practice. Highpass filtering
removes the DC component of the chaotic signal, which carries no information
and is blocked by AC-coupled channels. Bandpass filtering confines the signal
to an allocated band, and bandstop filtering creates a spectral notch to
avoid narrowband interference or to coexist with other
systems~\cite{Li2013uwb}. From a dynamical standpoint, these four types also span the
range of DC gains $G$ of the filter, which determines the fixed point of the
expanded system~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024}.

% [Items 19, 20] Paragraph 4: why the question is necessary and the gap
Inserting a filter in the feedback loop, however, changes the dynamics of the
generator, and nothing guarantees that the transmitted signal remains
chaotic. Once the unfiltered pair synchronizes, the filter preserves
synchronization for any choice of coefficients~\cite{BorgesTese2026}, but it
may destroy the chaotic behavior of the generator. Borges and Eisencraft
showed numerically that the coefficients determine whether the generated
signals are chaotic, periodic, or divergent, for a two-coefficient
filter~\cite{BorgesCSF2022} and for lowpass filters of increasing
order~\cite{Borges2022sbrt}, with bifurcation cascades, coexisting
attractors, crises, and shrimp-shaped periodic islands~\cite{Gallas1993}.
Borges \textit{et al.} extended this analysis to finite impulse response (FIR) filters with more
coefficients and different zero placements~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024} and to
second-order all-pole filters~\cite{Borges2024sbrt}. The preservation of
chaos must therefore be verified for each filter design. It remains open
which combinations of filter type, design window, order, and cutoff
frequency keep the generator chaotic, and this is the question addressed
here.

% [Item 21] Paragraph 5: what this work does (single paragraph; the list of
% contributions was moved to Section V)
Previous studies varied the coefficients of a two-coefficient
filter~\cite{BorgesCSF2022}, the zero placement of the FIR
filter~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024}, and the order and cutoff frequency of lowpass
filters designed with the Hamming and Blackman
windows~\cite{Borges2022sbrt,BorgesCNSNS2024}.
In practice, however, an FIR filter is specified by its frequency response
and designed by a method such as windowing, so the filter type and the design
window are natural design choices. This work investigates how these two
choices affect the preservation of chaos in the H\'enon map with an internal
FIR filter. Lowpass, highpass, bandpass, and bandstop filters are designed
with seven windows, including a sweep of the Kaiser shape
parameter~\cite{Kaiser1980}, and for each configuration the filter order and
the cutoff frequency are varied to build maps that classify the generated
signals as chaotic, periodic, or divergent. The parameters of the H\'enon map
and the bandwidth of the bandpass and bandstop filters are kept fixed.

% ---- II-A ----  [E-mail 7]: only the Hénon map, with a figure
\subsection{H\'enon Map}\label{sub:henon}
Consider the H\'enon map~\cite{Henon1976}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    x_1(n+1) = \alpha - x_1^2(n) + \beta\, x_2(n), \\
    x_2(n+1) = x_1(n),
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:henon}
\end{equation}
where $n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is the discrete time, $x_1(n)$ and $x_2(n)$ are
the state variables, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are real parameters. Grouping
the state variables in the vector $\mathbf{x}(n)=[x_1(n)\;\,x_2(n)]^{T}$,
where $(\cdot)^{T}$ denotes transposition, \eqref{eq:henon} can be written
compactly as $\mathbf{x}(n+1)=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(n))$. The sequence of
states $\{\mathbf{x}(n)\}_{n\ge 0}$ obtained by iterating $\mathbf{F}$ from an
initial condition $\mathbf{x}(0)$ is called an \emph{orbit}~\cite{Alligood1996}.

Depending on the parameters and on the initial condition, an orbit may
converge to a fixed point, converge to a periodic orbit, behave chaotically,
or diverge~\cite{Alligood1996}. A \emph{fixed point} is a state
$\mathbf{x}^\star$ left unchanged by one iteration,
$\mathbf{x}^\star=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^\star)$, so that an orbit started at
$\mathbf{x}^\star$ stays there for all $n$. An orbit is \emph{bounded} when
there is a finite $M$ such that $\|\mathbf{x}(n)\|\le M$ for all $n$, and
\emph{divergent} otherwise~\cite{Alligood1996}. For \eqref{eq:henon}, the
condition $\mathbf{x}^\star=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^\star)$ gives two fixed
points, of which the positive one is used as the reference state in this
work.

(a)~$\alpha=1.4$, $\beta=0.3$: chaotic signal. (b)~$\alpha=0.9$, $\beta=0.3$:

Fig.~\ref{fig:henon} illustrates the chaotic and the periodic behaviors with
segments of $x_1(n)$ generated from two initial conditions that differ by
$10^{-3}$ in $x_2(0)$, as in~\cite{Borges2022sbrt}. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:henon}(a), with $\alpha=1.4$ and $\beta=0.3$, the signal is
aperiodic and, although the two initial conditions are nearly
indistinguishable, the distance between them first exceeds $0.1$ at $n=11$
and reaches order one at $n=18$; this is the SDIC of chaotic signals. In Fig.~\ref{fig:henon}(b),
with $\alpha=0.9$ and $\beta=0.3$, both orbits converge to the same period-2
orbit, which alternates between $x_1\approx1.08$ and $x_1\approx-0.38$, and
the difference between the initial conditions is lost.
Throughout this work, $\alpha=1.4$ and $\beta=0.3$ are adopted; for these
classical values, \eqref{eq:henon} has a strange attractor of fractal
dimension $\approx1.26$~\cite{Sprott2003}.

\subsection{H\'enon Map With an FIR Filter in the Feedback Loop}\label{sub:expanded}
This subsection defines the filter, the expanded system obtained when the
filter is inserted in the map, and the quantities of that system used in the
rest of the paper. To limit the bandwidth of the generated signal, Eisencraft
\textit{et al.}~\cite{Eisencraft2009} and Fontes and
Eisencraft~\cite{Fontes2016} proposed filtering $x_1(n)$ with a finite
impulse response (FIR) filter and feeding the filter output back into the
map. The output of the filter is
\begin{equation}
  x_3(n) = \sum_{j=0}^{N_s-1} c_j\, x_1(n-j),
  \label{eq:filtro}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$, $0\le j\le N_s-1$, are the $N_s$ filter coefficients. The filter
order is $N_z=N_s-1$, equal to the number of zeros of the transfer function
obtained by taking the $z$-transform of~\eqref{eq:filtro} with zero initial
conditions~\cite{Oppenheim2009},
\begin{equation}
  H(z)=\frac{X_3(z)}{X_1(z)}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_s-1} c_j\, z^{-j}.
  \label{eq:tf}
\end{equation}

Evaluating $H(z)$ on the unit circle, $z=e^{j\omega}$, gives the frequency
response $H(e^{j\omega})$, where $\omega\in[0,\pi]$ is the normalized angular
frequency in rad/sample. The frequency response fixes the passband, the
transition width, and the stopband attenuation delivered by a given set of
coefficients~\cite{Oppenheim2009}. In this work, it is specified by the filter
type (lowpass, highpass, bandpass, or bandstop) and by a cutoff frequency
$\omega_c$. Its value at $\omega=0$ is the DC gain of the filter,
$G=H(e^{j0})=\sum_{j}c_j$~\cite{Oppenheim2009}.

The filtered signal $x_3(n)$ replaces $x_1(n)$ in the nonlinear term
of~\eqref{eq:henon}. Because~\eqref{eq:filtro} depends on the past samples
$x_1(n-1),\ldots,x_1(n-N_s+1)$, the pair $(x_1,x_2)$ is no longer a complete
state of the system. These samples are therefore stored as auxiliary state
variables $x_4,\ldots,x_{N_s}$, each holding a one-step delay of the
preceding one. For $N_s\ge3$, the resulting expanded system
is~\cite{Borges2022sbrt,BorgesCNSNS2024}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    x_1(n+1) = \alpha - x_3^2(n) + \beta\, x_2(n), \\
    x_2(n+1) = x_1(n), \\
    x_3(n+1) = c_0\bigl(\alpha - x_3^2(n) + \beta\, x_2(n)\bigr) + c_1\, x_1(n) \\
    \qquad\qquad + c_2\, x_2(n) + c_3\, x_4(n) + \cdots + c_{N_s-1}\, x_{N_s}(n), \\
    x_4(n+1) = x_2(n), \\
    x_j(n+1) = x_{j-1}(n),\quad j = 5,\ldots,N_s,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:expandido}
\end{equation}
whose dimension is $K=N_s$; for $N_s=3$, the terms in $x_4,\ldots,x_{N_s}$
are absent. For $N_s\le2$, the filter output $x_3(n)=c_0x_1(n)+c_1x_2(n)$ (with
$c_1=0$ for $N_s=1$) is a function of the current state, so no auxiliary
variable is needed and the system keeps dimension $K=2$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    x_1(n+1) = \alpha - \bigl(c_0x_1(n)+c_1x_2(n)\bigr)^2 + \beta\, x_2(n), \\
    x_2(n+1) = x_1(n).
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:expandido2}
\end{equation}
For $N_s=1$ and $c_0=1$, the filter is the identity and the original
map~\eqref{eq:henon} is recovered. Equations~\eqref{eq:expandido}
and~\eqref{eq:expandido2} are the forms implemented in the
package~\cite{chaoticPFC2026}.

As for~\eqref{eq:henon}, the fixed points of~\eqref{eq:expandido} are the
solutions of $\mathbf{x}^\star=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^\star)$, where
$\mathbf{F}$ now denotes the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:expandido}. At a
fixed point all delayed samples are equal, $x_1=x_2=x_4=\cdots=x_{N_s}=p$, and
$x_3=Gp$, which gives $G^2p^2+(1-\beta)p-\alpha=0$~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024,BorgesTese2026}.
Its positive root is written here as
\begin{equation}
  p^{+} = \frac{2\alpha}{(1-\beta)+\sqrt{(1-\beta)^2+4\alpha G^2}},
  \label{eq:pfixo}
\end{equation}
which is algebraically equal to the usual form
$\bigl[-(1-\beta)+\sqrt{(1-\beta)^2+4\alpha G^2}\bigr]/(2G^2)$ but avoids the
cancellation of that form when $G\to0$, as in highpass and bandpass filters,
for which~\eqref{eq:pfixo} tends to $\alpha/(1-\beta)$. The positive fixed
point is $\mathbf{p}^+=[p^+\;\,p^+\;\,Gp^+\;\,p^+\cdots p^+]^T$ (or
$[p^+\;\,p^+]^T$ for $N_s\le2$); it depends on the filter only through its DC
gain $G$ and is used to initialize the simulations
(Section~\ref{sub:estimator}).

\subsection{Largest Lyapunov Exponent}\label{sub:lyap}
The sensitivity of a system to its initial conditions can be quantified by
how fast two very close states move apart as they evolve. Lyapunov proposed
measuring this separation under the assumption that it grows exponentially,
so that the Lyapunov exponent is the natural logarithm of the average rate at
which two nearby orbits separate~\cite{Alligood1996,Borges2022sbrt}. An
exponent equal to $\ln2$ along a given direction, for instance, means that the
distance between the orbits doubles, on average, at each iteration in that
direction, whereas a negative exponent means that the orbits approach each
other. The SDIC of a chaotic signal is therefore measured by the Lyapunov
exponent: a computationally generated signal that is bounded, aperiodic, and
has at least one positive Lyapunov exponent is chaotic~\cite{Alligood1996}.

A map of dimension $K$ has $K$ Lyapunov exponents, one for each direction of
the state space, denoted in decreasing order by
$\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_K$. They are computed from the
\emph{Jacobian matrix} $\mathbf{J}(n)$, the $K\times K$ matrix of partial
derivatives $[\mathbf{J}(n)]_{ik}=\partial F_i/\partial x_k$ evaluated at
$\mathbf{x}(n)$, which describes how a small perturbation of the state is
transformed in one iteration~\cite{Alligood1996}. For the expanded
system~\eqref{eq:expandido}, it is~\cite{Borges2022sbrt,BorgesCNSNS2024}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J}(n) = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 & \beta & -2x_3(n) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    c_1 & c_0\beta + c_2 & -2c_0 x_3(n) & c_3 & \cdots & c_{N_s-1} \\
    0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & & & \ddots & & \vdots \\
    0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:jacobiana}
\end{equation}
in which only the third row depends on the filter coefficients; for
$N_s\le2$, $\mathbf{J}(n)$ is the $2\times2$ Jacobian
of~\eqref{eq:expandido2}. For the map~\eqref{eq:henon}, $\det\mathbf{J}(n)=-\beta$
for every $n$, so its two exponents satisfy
$\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\ln|\beta|$~\cite{Alligood1996}, an identity used in
Section~\ref{sub:validation} to validate the implementation.

The exponents are computed by the tangent-map
method~\cite{Alligood1996,Borges2022sbrt}. A hypersphere of unit radius
centered at $\mathbf{x}(0)$ is represented by a matrix $\mathbf{U}$ whose
columns are its orthogonal axes. Each iteration of the map turns the
hypersphere into an ellipsoid, and after $n$ iterations the axes of the
ellipsoid are the columns of
$\mathbf{J}(n-1)\cdots\mathbf{J}(1)\mathbf{J}(0)\mathbf{U}$. Computing the
lengths of these axes directly is numerically inadequate, because directions
that are stretched and directions that are compressed produce very large and
very small numbers. Instead, the growth of each axis is computed at every
iteration, and the axes are orthonormalized again before the next one, in the
following steps~\cite{Alligood1996}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose an orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{w}_1(0),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_K(0)\}$
  and compute $\mathbf{z}_k(0)=\mathbf{J}(0)\mathbf{w}_k(0)$, $1\le k\le K$.
  These vectors are axes of the new ellipsoid, but they are not necessarily
  orthogonal.
  \item Orthogonalize them by the Gram--Schmidt process,
  \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}_k(1)=\mathbf{z}_k(0)-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}
    \frac{\mathbf{z}_k(0)\cdot\mathbf{y}_i(1)}{\|\mathbf{y}_i(1)\|^2}\,
    \mathbf{y}_i(1),
    \label{eq:gs}
  \end{equation}
  where ``$\cdot$'' denotes the scalar product.
  \item Normalize the vectors, $\mathbf{w}_k(1)=\mathbf{y}_k(1)/\|\mathbf{y}_k(1)\|$,
  which gives the orthonormal basis for the next iteration.
  \item Apply the Jacobian at the next point of the orbit,
  $\mathbf{z}_k(1)=\mathbf{J}(1)\mathbf{w}_k(1)$, and repeat steps~2 and~3.
  After $n$ repetitions, the vectors $\mathbf{y}_k(1),\ldots,\mathbf{y}_k(n)$
  are available.
\end{enumerate}
The Lyapunov exponent in the $k$-th direction is then
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_k = \lim_{n\to\infty}
  \frac{\ln\|\mathbf{y}_k(1)\| + \cdots + \ln\|\mathbf{y}_k(n)\|}{n}.
  \label{eq:lyap}
\end{equation}

Only the largest exponent, $\lambda_1$, is used in this work. For
$\alpha=1.4$ and $\beta=0.3$, the map
\eqref{eq:henon} has
$\lambda_1\approx0.42$~\cite{Sprott2003}.

Based on these concepts, each orbit is classified as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \text{divergent}, & \text{if the orbit is unbounded},\\
    \text{chaotic},   & \text{if it is bounded and } \lambda_1 > 0, \\
    \text{periodic},  & \text{if it is bounded and } \lambda_1 \le 0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:criterio}
\end{equation}
where the periodic class also includes convergence to a fixed point, because
the criterion distinguishes only the presence or absence of chaos and not the
period of the limit orbit. The numerical detection of unbounded orbits is
described in Section~\ref{sub:metrics}.

\subsection{FIR Design Windows}\label{sub:windows}
This subsection describes how the coefficients $c_j$ are obtained and which
design windows are compared. The coefficients are obtained by the window
method~\cite{Oppenheim2009,Harris1978}: the impulse response of the ideal
filter, which is infinitely long, is conveniently delayed and truncated by a
window of $N_s$ samples. The shape of the window sets a trade-off between the
stopband attenuation and the width of the transition band of the resulting
filter: windows with lower side lobes give higher attenuation at the cost of
a wider transition band~\cite{Harris1978}.

Seven windows are considered (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The values listed
are the peak side-lobe levels of the discrete-time Fourier transform of each
window, relative to the main-lobe peak~\cite{Harris1978}; in the window
method, lower side lobes give higher stopband attenuation of the designed
filter~\cite{Oppenheim2009}. The values range from $-13$~dB for the
rectangular window, whose abrupt truncation produces strong side lobes, to
$-92$~dB for the four-term Blackman--Harris window, whose cosine-sum
coefficients are chosen to cancel side lobes~\cite{Harris1978}.

The seven windows cover a broad range of this trade-off: the rectangular
window as the worst case; the Bartlett and Hann windows as classical
intermediate cases; the Hamming window, adopted as a reference because it was
used in the previous works on the filtered H\'enon
map~\cite{Fontes2016,Borges2022sbrt}; and the Blackman and Blackman--Harris
windows as high-attenuation cases. They were chosen under the hypothesis that
greater spectral attenuation favors the preservation of chaos by reducing
side-lobe leakage into the rejected band. The Kaiser
window~\cite{Kaiser1980} has a shape parameter $\beta_K\ge0$ that varies the
trade-off continuously without changing the filter order: for $\beta_K=0$ it
is the rectangular window, and for $\beta_K\in[2,14]$ its peak side-lobe
level, computed here for a window of 101 samples, goes from about $-19$~dB to
$-106$~dB, a range that contains the side-lobe levels of the Bartlett, Hann,
Hamming, Blackman, and Blackman--Harris windows in
Table~\ref{tab:windows}. The sweep of $\beta_K$ therefore provides a
continuous path between the fixed windows.

\subsection{Synchronization Versus Chaos: Motivation}\label{sub:sync}
This subsection explains why a filter in the feedback loop is needed in
coherent CBCS and why its presence makes the verification of chaos necessary.
It is a motivation for the study: the sweeps of Sections~\ref{sec:method}
and~\ref{sec:results} do not transmit any message.

In a coherent CBCS of the master--slave type~\cite{Pecora1990}, let
$\mathbf{x}(n)$ and $\mathbf{y}(n)$ denote the states of the transmitter
(master) and of the receiver (slave), and let
$\mathbf{e}(n)\triangleq\mathbf{y}(n)-\mathbf{x}(n)$ be the synchronization
error. When the nonlinearity acts on a single scalar signal extracted from
the state, as $x_1(n)$ in~\eqref{eq:henon}, the error obeys the linear
recursion
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}(n+1) = \mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{e}(n),
  \label{eq:syncerror}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ is the synchronization matrix. Synchronization is attained
when every eigenvalue $\gamma_k$ of $\mathbf{A}$ lies strictly inside the unit
circle, $|\gamma_k|<1$~\cite{BorgesTese2026}. Over an ideal,
distortion-free channel, this condition is enough for the receiver to track
the transmitter and for the message to be recovered after an initial
transient.

Real channels, however, have a finite passband and act as filters that
reshape the spectrum of the transmitted signal~\cite{Eisencraft2011}. Because
chaotic carriers spread their energy continuously over a wide
band~\cite{Baptista2021}, even a moderate truncation of the spectrum makes the
slave evolve under a different effective nonlinearity, so the synchronization
error no longer vanishes and the recovered message no longer follows the
original one~\cite{BorgesTese2026}. Confining the transmitted spectrum to the
channel passband before transmission is therefore a precondition for coherent
detection over band-limited channels~\cite{Eisencraft2009,Fontes2016}.

In both, the message encodes the ASCII text ``hello'' (8 bits per character,

In Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_bl}, the channel is a 201-tap linear-phase lowpass FIR

channel gives a BER of 0 and a mean squared error of $2.0\times10^{-5}$

and are reported as motivation.

Inserting the filter in the feedback loop, rather than only at the channel
interface, also changes the synchronization matrix $\mathbf{A}$, as stated
next.

\begin{lemma}[Preservation of synchronization under FIR feedback filtering,
adapted from~\cite{BorgesTese2026}]\label{lem:sync}
Let the unfiltered master--slave pair satisfy the synchronization condition
$|\gamma_k|<1$ for every eigenvalue $\gamma_k$ of $\mathbf{A}$. Inserting
the same FIR filter in the feedback loop of the master and of the slave, as
in~\eqref{eq:expandido}, only adds null eigenvalues to the synchronization
matrix. Hence the original condition remains valid, independently of the
coefficients $c_j$~\cite{Fontes2016,BorgesTese2026}.
\end{lemma}

\textit{Proof sketch.} The nonlinearity of master and slave is driven by the
same transmitted signal, so it cancels in the error, and the filter states
(the filter output and its delay line) do not feed back into the error of the
original states. Ordering the error vector as [original states; filter
states], the synchronization matrix is block lower triangular, with diagonal
blocks $\mathbf{A}$ and a shift matrix of zero diagonal. Its eigenvalues are
therefore those of $\mathbf{A}$ together with zeros~\cite{BorgesTese2026}
(Theorem~3.1; the block-triangular argument is detailed for infinite impulse response (IIR) filters in
its Theorems~6.1 and~6.2).


By Lemma~\ref{lem:sync}, the filter preserves the synchronization of the
master--slave pair for any choice of coefficients, provided that the
unfiltered pair synchronizes. The lemma concerns only the linear stability of
the error recursion~\eqref{eq:syncerror}, however, and says nothing about
whether the master's own orbit remains chaotic once the same coefficients
enter the nonlinear dynamics~\eqref{eq:expandido} and its
Jacobian~\eqref{eq:jacobiana}. Synchronization and chaotic behavior are thus
independent requirements: the former is guaranteed by construction, whereas
the latter is not, as shown numerically
in~\cite{Borges2022sbrt,BorgesCSF2022,BorgesCNSNS2024}. A frequency response
$H(e^{j\omega})$ that fits the channel passband does not ensure, by itself,
that the filtered generator remains chaotic. The dynamics of each design must
be verified, which is the purpose of the methodology described next.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================
The research adopts a quantitative-experimental approach: for each filter
design, the expanded system~\eqref{eq:expandido} is simulated, $\lambda_1$ is
estimated, and each grid point is classified by~\eqref{eq:criterio}. All the
tools were implemented in the open-source Python package
\texttt{chaotic\_pfc} (BSD-3-Clause license)~\cite{chaoticPFC2026}.

% ---- III-A ----  (former III-B and III-C)
\subsection{Filter Types and Parameter Grid}\label{sub:grid}
Four filter types are designed with the seven windows of
Table~\ref{tab:windows} by the routine \texttt{firwin} of the SciPy
library~\cite{Virtanen2020}. For lowpass and highpass filters, $\omega_c$ is
the cutoff frequency; for bandpass and bandstop filters, it is the center of a
band of fixed total width $\Delta\omega=0.05\,\pi$, placed symmetrically around
$\omega_c$. On the normalized axis $\omega/\pi\in(0,1)$, this width is $5\%$
of the range and spans about five steps of the cutoff grid, so the band is
narrow enough to keep the filter frequency-selective and wide enough to be
resolved by the sweep at every $\omega_c$. Fixing $\Delta\omega$ also isolates
the effect of $\omega_c$; the width is left as a variable for future work
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

Because it is the frequency response, and not the individual coefficients,
that must be matched to the channel (Section~\ref{sub:sync}), the sweep
variables are the filter order $N_z$ and the cutoff frequency $\omega_c$. For
lowpass and bandpass filters, $N_z\in\{1,2,\ldots,40\}$ and 100 values of
$\omega_c/\pi$ linearly spaced in the open interval $(0,1)$ are swept, giving
$4{,}000$ grid points per window--filter configuration. For highpass and
bandstop filters, the parity restriction of the window method requires even
$N_z$~\cite{Oppenheim2009}, because a filter with an odd order has a forced
zero at $\omega=\pi$, which a passband that includes $\omega=\pi$ does not
allow; thus
$N_z\in\{2,4,\ldots,40\}$, with $2{,}000$ points per configuration. The six
fixed windows give $4\times6=24$ configurations, and the Kaiser window is
swept over 25 values of $\beta_K\in[2.0,14.0]$, with step $0.5$, for the four
filter types, adding 100 configurations, for a total of 124 sweeps and
$372{,}000$ grid points. For the Hann and Bartlett windows with lowpass and
bandpass filters, \texttt{firwin} returns non-finite coefficients for part of
the grid; these points are classified as divergent because of the filter
design, not of the map dynamics.

% ---- III-B ----  (former III-D and III-E)
\subsection{Lyapunov Estimator and Initial Conditions}\label{sub:estimator}
Computing all $K$ exponents by the method of Section~\ref{sub:lyap} costs
$\mathcal{O}(K^3)$ operations per iteration, dominated by the Gram--Schmidt
orthogonalization. Since only $\lambda_1$ is needed, the sweeps propagate a
single tangent vector, multiplied by $\mathbf{J}(n)$ at each iteration and
renormalized once every ten iterations, which reduces the cost to
$\mathcal{O}(K)$ per iteration. The formulation follows Wolf
\textit{et al.}~\cite{Wolf1985}, and the use of a renormalization period
follows Benettin \textit{et al.}~\cite{Benettin1980a,Benettin1980b}.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:lyap} summarizes the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Single-vector estimation of $\lambda_1$ with periodic renormalization.}\label{alg:lyap}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} initial condition $\mathbf{x}_0$; burn-in iterations $N_{\text{burn}}$; accumulation cap $N_{\text{map}}$; tolerance $\varepsilon$.
\STATE $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow[1\;0\;\cdots\;0]^T$;\; $s\leftarrow 0$;\; $\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}_0$.
\FOR{$i=1$ \TO $N_{\text{burn}}$}
  \STATE $\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})$ \quad(discard transient)
  \IF{$\mathbf{x}$ is non-finite}
    \STATE \textbf{return} DIVERGENT \quad(grid point)
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{$i=1$ \TO $N_{\text{map}}$}
  \STATE $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{w}$ \quad(tangent-vector evolution at the current state)
  \STATE $\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})$ \quad(orbit evolution)
  \IF{$\mathbf{x}$ is non-finite}
    \STATE \textbf{return} NaN \quad(divergent orbit)
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$i$ is a multiple of 10}
    \STATE $r\leftarrow\|\mathbf{w}\|$;\; $s\leftarrow s+\ln\max(r,10^{-300})$;\; if $r>10^{-300}$ then $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}/r$
    \STATE every 100 iterations with $i\ge500$: stop if $s/i$ varied less than $\varepsilon$ in two consecutive checks
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $\lambda_1=s/i$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

As in~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024}, $\lambda_1$ is taken as the largest of the $K$
finite-time exponents. To reduce the cost, a single tangent vector is
propagated first, and grid points whose mean estimate satisfies
$|\lambda_1|<5\times10^{-3}$ are recomputed with the full spectrum of
Section~\ref{sub:lyap}, using the modified Gram--Schmidt algorithm;
$34{,}147$ of the $372{,}000$ grid points ($9.18\%$) were recomputed.

For each grid point $(N_z,\omega_c)$, $N_{\text{IC}}=25$ initial conditions are
drawn near the positive fixed point $\mathbf{p}^+$ of the expanded system
(Section~\ref{sub:expanded}): each component is $p_k^++0.1\,U$, with $U$
uniformly distributed in $[0,1)$. For each initial condition,
$N_{\text{burn}}=500$ iterations are discarded as transient, and $\lambda_1$ is
accumulated for at most $N_{\text{map}}=3{,}000$ iterations. The value
assigned to the grid point is the mean of $\lambda_1$ over the initial
conditions.

The value $N_{\text{burn}}=500$ is much longer than the transients of the
unfiltered map and accommodates longer transients near bifurcation
boundaries. The value
$N_{\text{map}}$ acts as a cap: with early stopping, the median number of
accumulated iterations per configuration lies between about $700$ and
$1{,}200$, with a floor of $700$, and the cap is reached in at most $0.06\%$ of
the points, in only two of the 124 configurations. In all experiments,
$\alpha=1.4$, $\beta=0.3$,
and the seed of the pseudo-random generator is 42.

% ---- III-C ----  (former III-F)
\subsection{Classification and Chaotic-Coverage Metrics}\label{sub:metrics}
Each grid point is classified by~\eqref{eq:criterio}. Numerically, an orbit is
taken as unbounded when its state becomes non-finite (NaN or infinity); from
that iteration on, every term of~\eqref{eq:lyap} is non-finite, so the
simulation can be stopped. The divergence rule has three steps. First, the
estimator returns a non-finite value for an initial condition as soon as its
state becomes non-finite. Second, a grid point is divergent when at least one
of its 25 initial conditions becomes non-finite during the burn-in. Third, an
initial condition that becomes non-finite only after the burn-in is
discarded, and the value of the grid point is the mean of $\lambda_1$ over the
remaining initial conditions. This step differs from~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024}, in
which a grid point is divergent when any of its initial conditions becomes
non-finite. Points with non-finite filter coefficients
(Section~\ref{sub:grid}) are also divergent.

Let $N_{\text{grid}}$ be the number of grid points of a configuration, $N_b$
the number of bounded (chaotic or periodic) points, and $N_c$ the number of
chaotic points. The \emph{conditional} fraction
$\rho_c^{\text{fin}}=N_c/N_b$ is the share of chaotic points among the bounded
ones; it measures the tendency to chaos once divergence is excluded and is
relevant when $N_z$ and $\omega_c$ can be adjusted to reach a non-divergent
operating point. The \emph{absolute} fraction
$\rho_c^{\text{abs}}=N_c/N_{\text{grid}}$ is the share of the whole design
space that yields a chaotic generator and is relevant when a broad operating
range is sought. Using fractions also makes the comparison between filter
types independent of their different grid sizes (Section~\ref{sub:grid}).

% ---- III-D ----  (former III-G)
\subsection{Statistical Treatment}\label{sub:stats}
Two levels are kept separate. At the \emph{point} level, the value of
$\lambda_1$ of each grid point is the mean over its $N_{\text{IC}}$ initial
conditions, and the dispersion over the ensemble is stored as metadata. At the
\emph{regional} level, the chaotic region of each configuration is summarized
by the mean $\bar\lambda_1$ and the standard deviation $\sigma_{\lambda_1}$ of
$\lambda_1$ over its $N_c$ chaotic points; thus $\sigma_{\lambda_1}$ measures
how homogeneous $\lambda_1$ is across the chaotic region, not the estimation
noise at one point. A $95\%$ confidence interval for $\bar\lambda_1$ is
obtained by nonparametric bootstrap~\cite{EfronTibshirani1993} with $1{,}000$
resamples and seed 42. These regional statistics are the ones reported in
Tables~\ref{tab:best} and~\ref{tab:ranking}. Because the chaotic points are
selected by $\lambda_1>0$, this interval is positive by construction, and
because neighboring grid points are correlated, the i.i.d.\ bootstrap
underestimates the uncertainty; the interval is therefore used only to
describe dispersion. The robustness of the classification is assessed by
three other checks: the fraction of chaotic points whose ensemble mean of
$\lambda_1$ exceeds two standard errors, $2s/\sqrt{N_{\text{IC}}}$, where $s$
is the standard deviation of $\lambda_1$ over the initial conditions of the
point; the sensitivity of $N_c$ to the classification threshold ($0$,
$10^{-3}$, and $5\times10^{-3}$); and the repetition of the configurations of
Table~\ref{tab:best} with ten seeds of the initial-condition generator (1 to
9 and 42).

% ---- III-E ----  (former III-A and III-H, plus file paths from former IV-G)
\subsection{Computational Architecture and Reproducibility}\label{sub:impl}
Reliable estimates of $\lambda_1$ require long simulations and an ensemble of
initial conditions~\cite{Wolf1985}, and each sweep has thousands of grid
points: a $40\times100$ grid with 25 initial conditions requires $10^5$ runs of
the estimator. For the full-spectrum recomputation, the cost per grid point is
$\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{IC}}\cdot N_{\text{map}}\cdot K^3)$, which for $N_z=40$
($K=41$) approaches $5\times10^{9}$ floating-point operations. Three decisions
make the study tractable. First, the numerical cores are compiled just in time
by Numba~\cite{Numba}, which translates Python functions into machine code
through LLVM and reduced the execution time by one to two orders of magnitude
relative to pure Python in the package benchmarks~\cite{chaoticPFC2026}.
Second, the grid points are processed in parallel on all processor cores with
\texttt{prange} loops. Third, each sweep is stored as a compressed NumPy
(\texttt{.npz}) file, so that figures and tables can be regenerated without
repeating the sweeps.

Each \texttt{.npz} file contains the ensemble mean of $\lambda_1$ per grid
point and its standard deviation, the filter orders and coefficients, the
cutoff grid, the window and filter-type labels, the number of iterations
actually used, and the simulation parameters ($N_{\text{burn}}$,
$N_{\text{map}}$, number of initial conditions, $\alpha$, $\beta$, seed,
$\beta_K$, early-stop settings, and number of recomputed points). The files
are versioned in \texttt{data/sweeps/}; the figures are generated by
\path{src/chaotic_pfc/analysis/sweep_plotting.py} and the statistics by
\path{chaotic_pfc/analysis/stats.py}. Automated tests in \texttt{tests/},
run by continuous integration at every change of the code, verify the
identity $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\ln|\beta|$ for~\eqref{eq:henon}, the value of
$\lambda_1$ against published references~\cite{Wolf1985,Sprott2003}, and the
designed filters (coefficient sum and zero placement). The package has a Git
version tag, a \texttt{CITATION.cff} file following the FORCE11
recommendations for software citation, and Sphinx documentation published on
Read the Docs~\cite{chaoticPFC2026}.

\subsection{Validation of the Estimator}\label{sub:validation}
The estimator was first validated on the map~\eqref{eq:henon} without filter
($N_s=1$, $c_0=1$), with $\alpha=1.4$ and $\beta=0.3$, for which
$\lambda_1\approx0.42$~\cite{Wolf1985,Sprott2003}. With the

$\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\ln|\beta|$ (Section~\ref{sub:lyap}) was satisfied to
machine precision in all unfiltered configurations tested, which confirms the
correctness of the estimator independently of the reference value. This
validation used the full-spectrum estimator; the sweeps use the single-vector
path of Algorithm~\ref{alg:lyap}, with early stopping.

points and the Hamming window $450$ (Table~\ref{tab:ranking}). The two studies

initial conditions (10 drawn in $[0,1]$ in~\cite{Borges2022sbrt}, 25 drawn
near $\mathbf{p}^+$ here), and in the comparison criterion (curves of
$\lambda_1$ there, counts of chaotic points here). The lowpass filter with
the Hamming window also corresponds to Experiment~V of~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024},
which swept the same range of $N_z$.

remains close to one. The lowpass filter with the Kaiser window
($\beta_K=13.5$) reaches the highest absolute coverage among the 124
configurations, $\rho_c^{\text{abs}}=24.5\%$, about $4.2$ times that of the
rectangular bandstop filter, or $8.3$ times in number of chaotic points ($979$
against $118$).

% ---- IV-C ----
\subsection{Classification Maps}\label{sub:maps}
Figs.~\ref{fig:lowpass}--\ref{fig:bandstop} show the classification maps in
the $(N_z,\omega_c/\pi)$ plane for the configuration of largest $N_c$ of each
filter type, with chaotic points in red, periodic points in blue, and
divergent points in gray. In the lowpass map (Fig.~\ref{fig:lowpass}), chaos
lies below a boundary in $\omega_c/\pi$ that decreases with $N_z$, and the
orbits diverge above it. For $N_z$ from 3 to 7, chaotic points reach
$\omega_c/\pi\approx1$ (up to $0.78$ for $N_z=4$), whereas the largest
chaotic cutoff is $0.44$ for $N_z=10$, $0.14$ for $N_z=20$, and $0.06$ for
$N_z=40$; for $N_z=1$ and $2$ no point is chaotic. In the highpass map
(Fig.~\ref{fig:highpass}), chaos concentrates at $\omega_c/\pi\le0.26$ for
$N_z\ge6$ and spans the whole cutoff range only for $N_z=4$
($0.38\le\omega_c/\pi\le1$); the largest $\lambda_1$, $0.106$, occurs at
$N_z=6$ and $\omega_c/\pi=0.01$, the lower edge of the swept interval. The
lowpass and highpass filters therefore differ mainly in how the chaotic
region depends on $N_z$: at high orders, both have chaos only at low cutoff
frequencies. The bandpass map (Fig.~\ref{fig:bandpass}) shows a fragmented
chaotic region, more sensitive to $\omega_c$, which is consistent with the
narrow band imposed by the filter. In the bandstop map
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bandstop}), chaos appears in isolated bands and divergence
dominates the grid: only $N_z=2$ has no divergent points, and for $N_z=4$, 8,
12, and 16 the whole row diverges.

These maps are consistent with two observations
of~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024,BorgesCSF2022}. First, the DC gain $G$ sets the
positive fixed point~\eqref{eq:pfixo}, so filter types with different $G$
place the orbits in different regions of the state
space~\cite{BorgesCNSNS2024}. Second, the thin boundaries between chaotic and
periodic regions and the periodic islands inside the chaotic regions resemble
the period-doubling cascades and the shrimps~\cite{Gallas1993} documented for
the filtered H\'enon map by Borges and Eisencraft~\cite{BorgesCSF2022}; such
structures appear in the four filter types studied here.

The filter types also differ in how the grid is divided among the three
classes. Pooling the 124 sweeps, the numbers of chaotic, periodic, and
divergent points are: lowpass, $20{,}906$, $17{,}701$, and $85{,}393$ out of
$124{,}000$ ($68.87\%$ divergent); highpass, $2{,}900$, $54{,}902$, and
$4{,}198$ out of $62{,}000$ ($6.77\%$); bandpass, $10{,}219$, $100{,}990$, and
$12{,}791$ out of $124{,}000$ ($10.32\%$); and bandstop, $1{,}598$, $3{,}035$,
and $57{,}367$ out of $62{,}000$ ($92.53\%$). Chaos is therefore limited
mainly by divergence in lowpass and bandstop filters and mainly by periodic
orbits in highpass and bandpass filters. % [Item 13]
Aggregated over all its configurations, the bandstop filter has
$\rho_c^{\text{fin}}=34.5\%$ ($1{,}598$ of $4{,}633$ bounded points), below
the lowpass filter ($54.2\%$) and above the bandpass ($9.2\%$) and highpass
($5.0\%$) filters; the value of $65.2\%$ is specific to the rectangular
window. The divergent fraction of $92.53\%$ of the bandstop filter is a result
of this study and does not constitute a recommendation for the use of this
filter type.

% ---- IV-D ----
\subsection{Aggregate Chaotic Density}\label{sub:density}
To identify the regions of the $(N_z,\omega_c/\pi)$ plane that are chaotic
regardless of the design, the chaotic points of the 124 sweeps were
aggregated. Because highpass and bandstop filters have only even $N_z$, a
grid point with even $N_z$ belongs to 124 configurations and one with odd
$N_z$ to 62 (lowpass and bandpass only). Fig.~\ref{fig:density} therefore
shows, at each grid point, the percentage of the configurations containing it
in which it is chaotic.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includesvg[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/fig_chaotic_density_norm}
\caption{Aggregate chaotic density over the 124 sweeps: percentage of the
configurations containing each grid point in which it is chaotic (124
configurations for even $N_z$, 62 for odd $N_z$, which have only lowpass and
bandpass filters). The marker ($\circ$) indicates the maximum, $80.6\%$ at
$N_z=3$ and $\omega_c/\pi\approx0.34$.}
\label{fig:density}
\end{figure}

For $N_z\ge8$, chaos is most frequent at low cutoff frequencies: on average,
a grid point is chaotic in $31.3\%$ of the configurations for
$\omega_c/\pi\le0.1$, in $16.8\%$ for $0.1<\omega_c/\pi\le0.2$, in $5.9\%$
for $0.2<\omega_c/\pi\le0.4$, and in less than $1.5\%$ above
$\omega_c/\pi=0.4$. For $N_z\le7$, the fraction varies less with the cutoff,
from $19.8\%$ to $29.4\%$. The maximum, $80.6\%$, occurs at $N_z=3$ and
$\omega_c/\pi\approx0.34$ (50 of 62 configurations); among even orders, it
occurs at $N_z=10$ and $\omega_c/\pi\approx0.11$ (71 of 124, $57.3\%$).
Odd and even orders are not directly comparable, because the odd ones include
only the lowpass and bandpass filters, which have more chaotic points; this
explains the alternation between neighboring columns in
Fig.~\ref{fig:density}.

The choice of window matters mainly at low and moderate orders. The six fixed
windows give the same class (chaotic, periodic, or divergent) at $25\%$ to
$29\%$ of the lowpass grid points for $N_z\le10$ and at $88\%$ to $91\%$ for
$N_z>20$; the corresponding values are $39\%$--$58\%$ and $86\%$--$90\%$ for
bandpass, $57\%$--$83\%$ and $93\%$--$94\%$ for highpass, and $78\%$--$82\%$
and $97\%$--$98\%$ for bandstop (points with non-finite coefficients count as
divergent). This convergence is expected, because the designed filters
approach the ideal one as $N_z$ grows~\cite{Oppenheim2009}.

Because the regime boundaries are finely interleaved, with period-doubling
cascades alternating with shrimps at progressively smaller
scales~\cite{Gallas1993,BorgesCSF2022}, a small change of $N_z$ or $\omega_c$
can move a design across a boundary. We therefore suggest, without having
measured it here, that the distance from an operating point to the nearest
boundary be considered in the design.

% ---- IV-E ----
\subsection{Effect of the Kaiser Parameter $\beta_K$}\label{sub:beta}
The values of $\beta_K$ that maximize $N_c$ (Table~\ref{tab:best}) are $13.5$
for lowpass and bandpass filters, $11.5$ for highpass, and $4.0$ for bandstop.
For the first three types, the maximum lies at high $\beta_K$, where the side
lobes of the window are strongly attenuated~\cite{Oppenheim2009}, which is
consistent with the advantage of the Blackman--Harris window among the fixed
windows (Section~\ref{sub:classification}). Fig.~\ref{fig:beta} shows
$\rho_c^{\text{abs}}$ versus $\beta_K$. For the lowpass filter,
$\rho_c^{\text{abs}}$ grows from $7.1\%$ at $\beta_K=2$ to $23.0\%$ at
$\beta_K=10$ and stays between $22.9\%$ and $24.5\%$ up to $\beta_K=14$. For
the bandpass filter, it grows from $4.0\%$ to values between $9.9\%$ and
$10.9\%$ for $\beta_K\ge10$, and for the highpass filter, from $2.0\%$ to a
maximum of $7.5\%$ at $\beta_K=11.5$, followed by values between $6.4\%$ and
$7.0\%$. Differences between neighboring values on these plateaus, such as
$973$ and $979$ chaotic points for the lowpass filter at $\beta_K=13.0$ and
$13.5$, are of the order of the seed variability (Section~\ref{sub:classification});
the $\beta_K$ of largest $N_c$ in Table~\ref{tab:best} is therefore one value
inside a plateau rather than a sharp optimum.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includesvg[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/fig_kaiser_beta}
\caption{Absolute chaotic coverage $\rho_c^{\text{abs}}$ versus the Kaiser
shape parameter $\beta_K$ for the four filter types.}
\label{fig:beta}
\end{figure}

For the bandstop filter, the largest $N_c$ occurs with the rectangular window
and, among the Kaiser configurations, at $\beta_K=4.0$, but its curve in
Fig.~\ref{fig:beta} is nearly flat, between $2.0\%$ and $2.9\%$. One
hypothesis, not tested here, is that, when the filter removes only a narrow
band of the spectrum, preserving most of the spectral energy in the feedback
loop matters more than the depth of the stopband attenuation, which would
agree with the result of the rectangular window, of lowest attenuation, for
this filter type. The
effect of $\beta_K$ is therefore not the same for the four filter types; it
depends on the spectral mask imposed.

\subsection{Statistical Confidence and Ranking}\label{sub:ranking}
The $95\%$ intervals of $\bar\lambda_1$ in Tables~\ref{tab:best}
and~\ref{tab:ranking} describe the dispersion of $\lambda_1$ over each chaotic
region and are positive by construction (Section~\ref{sub:stats}). The
classification itself is supported by two checks. First, $91.8\%$ of the
$35{,}623$ chaotic points of the 124 sweeps have an ensemble mean of
$\lambda_1$ above two standard errors (from $87.9\%$ to $98.3\%$ in the
configurations of Table~\ref{tab:best}). Second, $N_c$ depends on the
threshold: raising it from 0 to $10^{-3}$ and to $5\times10^{-3}$ keeps
$73.1\%$ and $56.9\%$ of the chaotic points, respectively, so many chaotic
points have small positive $\lambda_1$ and lie near the chaotic--periodic
boundary. The dispersion $\sigma_{\lambda_1}$ is highest for the rectangular bandstop filter
($0.0491$), whose fragmented chaotic region mixes low and high values of
$\lambda_1$, and smaller for the lowpass filter with the Kaiser window
($0.0231$), whose chaotic region is more homogeneous.

\subsection{Computational Cost}\label{sub:cost}
The 124 sweeps comprise $372{,}000$ grid points. The effective accumulation
horizons recorded per grid point add up to about $2\times10^{8}$ iterations,
that is, about $5\times10^{9}$ iterations of the expanded map once the 25
initial conditions of each point are counted. On an 8th-generation Intel Core
i7-8550U processor (4 physical cores, 8 threads, 32\,GB of RAM), the complete
set of sweeps took $489$\,s, about $8$\,min, measured end to end. Any figure or
table of this paper can be reproduced by running the corresponding script over
the versioned sweep files (Section~\ref{sub:impl}), without recomputing the
sweeps.

\section{Final Remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================
This work investigated numerically how the filter type and the design window
affect the preservation of chaos in the H\'enon map with an FIR filter in the
feedback loop. Four filter types (lowpass, highpass, bandpass, and bandstop)
and seven windows (rectangular, Bartlett, Hamming, Hann, Blackman,
Blackman--Harris, and Kaiser, with a sweep of its shape parameter $\beta_K$)
were combined in 124 sweeps of the order--cutoff plane.

The insertion of the filter can change the generated signals from chaotic to
periodic or divergent, depending on the pair $(N_z,\omega_c)$. The chaotic
regime is not eliminated by filtering, but reorganized into regions of the
parameter space whose shape depends on the filter type and the window. For
lowpass and bandpass filters, windows of high attenuation (Kaiser with high
$\beta_K$ and Blackman--Harris) give the largest chaotic coverage, with
$\rho_c^{\text{abs}}=24.5\%$ for the lowpass filter with the Kaiser window,
the highest among all configurations. For the bandstop filter, the rectangular
window gives the highest conditional coverage among its configurations
($\rho_c^{\text{fin}}=65.2\%$),
although its absolute coverage is only $5.9\%$. The divergent fraction of the
grid ranges from $6.77\%$ (highpass) and $10.32\%$ (bandpass) to $68.87\%$
(lowpass) and $92.53\%$ (bandstop). For $N_z\ge8$, chaos is most frequent
at low cutoff frequencies ($\omega_c/\pi\le0.1$), and the choice of window
matters mainly at low and moderate orders.

% [E-mail 5 / Item 21] Contributions stated here, in prose.
The work makes three contributions. First, it extends the previous studies of
the filtered H\'enon map~\cite{Borges2022sbrt,BorgesCSF2022,BorgesCNSNS2024},
in which the filters designed by windowing were lowpass filters with the
Hamming or the Blackman window, to four filter types and seven design windows, with classification maps that
show where chaos is preserved in each case. Second, it assesses the robustness of
the classification with the ensemble standard error of $\lambda_1$, the
sensitivity of $N_c$ to the classification threshold, and repeated runs with
ten seeds, which extend the point estimates reported in those studies. Third, it releases an open-source package~\cite{chaoticPFC2026} with
which all the maps and tables can be reproduced and the study extended.

Taken together, the results show that the joint choice of filter type and
design window is an important design factor of the filtered H\'enon
generator, especially at low and moderate orders, where the six fixed windows
disagree on the class of up to $75\%$ of the lowpass grid points. Because of the finely interleaved regime boundaries and of
the shrimps inside the chaotic regions~\cite{Gallas1993}, the design of a
filtered generator for CBCS cannot start from the spectral specification of
the filter alone: the dynamics of the resulting generator must be verified
explicitly, for example with classification maps such as the ones presented
here.

The study has limitations. It covers the filter order, the cutoff frequency,
the filter type, and the design window; other dimensions of the parameter
space were not swept. The sweeps are limited to $N_z\le40$ and
$N_{\text{map}}\le3{,}000$ iterations, and higher orders or longer horizons
could reveal other structures. The band width $\Delta\omega=0.05\,\pi$ of the
bandpass and bandstop filters was fixed, and its variation may change the
conclusions for these two types. The map parameters $(\alpha,\beta)=(1.4,0.3)$
were not swept, and other pairs of the parameter space of the H\'enon
map~\cite{Gallas1993} may behave differently under filtering. Finally, initial
conditions that diverge after the burn-in are excluded from the mean of
$\lambda_1$, which is therefore estimated over the surviving initial
conditions.

Three directions are proposed for future work: (a)~sweeping the band width
$\Delta\omega$ as an independent variable for bandpass and bandstop filters,
to identify transitions not captured in this study; (b)~extending the
methodology to IIR filters, whose dynamic
behavior is still little explored in the literature~\cite{Borges2024sbrt},
with explicit treatment of the stability of the filter itself; and
(c)~measuring the occupied bandwidth, the spectral leakage, and the in-band
power of the chaotic configurations identified, as metrics complementary to
$\lambda_1$ for the choice of operating points in CBCS.
